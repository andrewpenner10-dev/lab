# Experiment 003 — Laser + Photodiode Link

Date: September 29, 2026

## Goal

Upgrade the optical communication system from an LED and LDR to a laser transmitter and photodiode receiver.

Experiment 002 proved that two independent Arduino boards could transmit digital information through free-space light.

The next step was to replace the slow, diffuse optical hardware with components better suited to a real optical communications system:

Arduino TX → laser → free space → photodiode → Arduino RX

## Starting Point

The previous system used:

- Green LED transmitter
- LDR receiver
- Two Arduino Uno boards
- Software synchronization and binary encoding

That system successfully transmitted digital data, but the LDR was slow and the LED spread light in many directions.

For this experiment, I replaced both.

The new transmitter uses a 650 nm laser module.

The new receiver uses a BPW34 photodiode.

I also started referring to the two Arduino boards as:

- **101 — transmitter**
- **111 — receiver**

## Step 1 — Build the Laser Transmitter

I connected the laser module to Arduino 101.

The laser has three connections:

- Red — power
- Black — ground
- Yellow — TTL modulation

The yellow modulation wire is connected to Arduino digital pin 8.

This allows the Arduino to control the laser in software.

Instead of merely powering the laser continuously, Arduino 101 can switch the beam on and off electronically.

That gives me the basic mechanism needed to eventually encode digital information into the laser beam.

## Step 2 — Build the Photodiode Receiver

On Arduino 111, I replaced the LDR with a BPW34 photodiode.

The photodiode is connected to analog input A0 with a 10 kΩ resistor.

Arduino 111 continuously reads the analog voltage and prints the measurement to the Serial Monitor.

Under normal room lighting, the receiver measured approximately:

`16–18`

This established the baseline.

## Step 3 — Detect the Laser

I aimed the laser directly at the BPW34.

The result was immediate.

The reading jumped from approximately:

`17`

to:

`1023`

1023 is the maximum value of the Arduino Uno's 10-bit analog-to-digital converter.

The laser therefore produced a signal large enough to drive the receiver all the way to the top of its measurable range.

This was the first successful test of the new optical link:

Arduino 101 → laser → free space → BPW34 → Arduino 111

## Step 4 — Add Manual Laser Control

With the laser working, I wanted a faster and more deliberate way to control it during testing.

Previously, stopping the laser meant disconnecting power or changing the program.

I added a momentary pushbutton to Arduino 101.

The Arduino uses its internal pull-up resistor to read the button:

- Button released → digital input HIGH → laser OFF
- Button pressed → digital input LOW → laser ON

The Arduino then controls the laser through digital pin 8.

The result is a simple hold-to-emit control:

`RELEASED → LASER OFF`

`PRESSED → LASER ON`

This makes bench testing much easier and gives me direct physical control over when the laser is emitting.

## Current Prototype

The optical hardware is now:

Arduino 101 → 650 nm laser → free-space optical channel → BPW34 photodiode → Arduino 111

The transmitter can:

- Switch the laser electronically
- Be controlled manually with a pushbutton
- Eventually modulate the laser with encoded data

The receiver can:

- Detect the laser
- Measure its intensity
- Clearly distinguish the laser from ambient light

At short range, the received laser signal is currently strong enough to saturate the Arduino analog input.

## What Changed

Experiment 002 proved that information could cross the gap using light.

Experiment 003 replaces the original learning hardware with the beginnings of a more serious optical system.

The transition is:

LED → laser

LDR → photodiode

Diffuse light → directional beam

Slow light sensor → faster photodetector

Hand-positioned experiment → beginning of a controllable optical terminal

## Problems Discovered

The stronger optical hardware introduced a new set of engineering problems.

The biggest one is now **pointing**.

The laser beam is narrow enough that physical alignment matters significantly. The laser module is currently connected by relatively stiff wires, so without being mechanically secured it can move and point in unpredictable directions.

The receiver also needs to remain precisely aligned with the beam.

The photodiode signal currently saturates at close range, which means later experiments will also need to characterize receiver gain and dynamic range rather than simply maximizing the received signal.

## Next

The next phase will focus on turning the loose optical components into a controllable optical terminal.

The priorities are:

- Build a rigid laser mounting system.
- Add precise two-axis pointing control.
- Explore motorized azimuth and elevation.
- Eventually add closed-loop optical tracking.
- Characterize the photodiode receiver at increasing distances.
- Determine when a transimpedance amplifier is required.
- Resume digital modulation using the laser instead of the LED.
- Increase the communication speed beyond the previous LDR limit.

The communication principle was proven in Experiment 002.

Now the hardware is beginning to look like the system I actually want to build.
