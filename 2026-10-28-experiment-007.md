# Experiment 007 — Bidirectional Lasers, Hardware UART, and 200,000 bit/s

**Date: October 28, 2026**

## Goal

In Experiment 006, I used my Arduino-based laser communication system to transmit a real computing request, execute the calculation, and display the result on a locally hosted website.

The optical link was operating at 20,000 bit/s, and the system had successfully brought together three capabilities I want to continue developing: optical communication, computing, and internet infrastructure.

But there were two important limitations.

First, communication was still one-way. Appa transmitted information through the laser, and Momo received it.

Second, both Arduinos relied heavily on software to control the precise timing of individual bits.

For Experiment 007, I wanted to make progress on both problems:

1. Enable Appa and Momo to transmit and receive information in both directions.
2. Introduce hardware UART to improve communication performance.
3. Continue increasing the signalling rate until I discovered what was actually limiting the system.

By the end of the experiment, I had achieved bidirectional communication, successfully introduced hardware UART reception, and increased the verified optical signalling rate from 20,000 to **200,000 bit/s**.

I also encountered the first clear transmitter speed limit.

## Step 1 — Make Communication Bidirectional

Until now, Appa had been the transmitter and Momo had been the receiver.

I wanted both Arduinos to have the same capabilities.

I connected a second laser and configured the system so that either board could send information to the other.

This meant Appa could transmit to Momo, and Momo could transmit to Appa.

The first challenge was to establish reliable communication in both directions at the existing 20,000 bit/s signalling rate.

On October 23, I conducted a series of physical tests.

The results included:

- Momo → Appa: 22 of 22 verified packets.
- Appa → Momo: 18 of 20 packets overall, with the first two missing and the subsequent tests improving.
- Eight alternating transmission pairs: 8 of 8 packets verified in each direction.
- Zero CRC failures or rejected packets during the alternating eight-pair test.

The alternating tests were particularly satisfying.

Appa transmitted a packet to Momo, then Momo transmitted a packet back to Appa. This repeated across eight different payload patterns.

Every packet in that alternating sequence arrived correctly.

For the first time, I had two Arduino boards that could exchange data through laser light in both directions.

This was not yet full-duplex communication, where both sides transmit simultaneously. It was bidirectional, turn-taking communication.

But it was an important step toward building an actual optical network rather than a single one-way data link.

## Step 2 — Introduce Hardware UART

With bidirectional communication working, I turned my attention to speed.

Until now, my Arduino firmware had largely handled bit timing through software.

When transmitting a bit, the program controlled the output signal and waited for the appropriate time before sending the next one.

On the receiving side, software had to sample the incoming electrical signal at carefully chosen intervals.

That approach had worked remarkably well, but it required increasingly precise timing as the communication rate increased.

I wanted to investigate whether dedicated hardware inside the Arduino could perform some of that work instead.

This brought me to UART.

**UART stands for Universal Asynchronous Receiver/Transmitter.**

It's a hardware peripheral built into the Arduino Uno's microcontroller that can transmit and receive serial data without requiring the main processor to manually time every individual bit.

UART uses a defined frame structure, typically including:

- A start bit
- Eight data bits
- A stop bit

The transmitter and receiver agree on a communication speed, and the hardware handles the bit timing.

UART is usually associated with electrical communication between devices. But in principle, it doesn't matter whether the underlying data travelled through a wire or through light, provided the receiving hardware gets a correctly timed electrical signal.

That made it an interesting fit for my optical system.

I configured Appa to receive optical data through its hardware UART.

Momo continued generating the UART waveform in software, using its D8 output to drive the laser.

This let me change one part of the communication system at a time.

The new arrangement was:

**Momo → Software UART waveform → Laser → Photodiode receiver → Appa hardware UART**

## Step 3 — First Successful Hardware UART Transmission

On October 28, I performed the first physical test with Appa receiving through hardware UART.

The signalling rate was 20,000 bit/s.

I transmitted 64 packets using eight different test patterns.

All 64 packets arrived and passed verification.

There were:

- Zero detected payload errors
- Zero CRC failures
- Zero framing errors
- Zero overruns
- Zero duplicate or incomplete packets

I reset both Arduinos and repeated the test without changing the configuration.

The second run also passed.

Across the two tests, Appa successfully received **128 of 128 verified packets**, with no detected errors.

The estimated useful payload throughput was approximately 7,275 bit/s, including packet overhead and gaps.

That was considerably higher than the roughly 4,000 bit/s of useful payload throughput measured in some of my earlier software-timed experiments, although the test configurations and overhead were different.

The important achievement was that Appa's hardware UART could successfully reconstruct bytes delivered through the laser.

I now had a working hardware-based optical receiver.

## Step 4 — Increase the Speed

With the first tests passing, I began increasing the signalling rate.

I wanted to determine how fast the system could operate without losing packets.

Rather than making several changes simultaneously, I preserved the successful versions and tested higher speeds separately.

The progression was:

| Signalling rate | Reception result | Useful payload throughput |
|---|---|---|
| 20,000 bit/s | 128/128 verified | ~7.3 kb/s |
| 31,250 bit/s | 128/128 verified | — |
| 57,143 bit/s | 128/128 verified | ~12.8 kb/s |
| 100,000 bit/s | 128/128 verified | ~15.8 kb/s |
| 200,000 bit/s | 128/128 verified | ~18.7 kb/s |
| 250,000 bit/s | Transmitter scheduling fault | No completed packet |

Each successful rate was tested in two short physical runs.

The 100,000 bit/s milestone was particularly exciting.

At that speed, each bit lasts only 10 microseconds.

Momo had to generate the waveform through software while Appa's hardware UART reconstructed the incoming bytes.

Both 100,000 bit/s tests passed without detected reception errors.

I decided to attempt 200,000 bit/s.

## Step 5 — Push Beyond the Laser Module's Advertised Modulation Limit

My current laser is an Adafruit 650 nm TTL-controlled module.

Its published specifications list a maximum TTL modulation frequency of 50 kHz.

For an alternating sequence of ones and zeros, a 100,000 bit/s signal corresponds to approximately 50,000 complete on/off cycles per second.

That placed my 100,000 bit/s test around the module's advertised modulation frequency.

At 200,000 bit/s, alternating bits would require approximately 100,000 on/off cycles per second.

That's twice the published frequency.

I wanted to see what would happen.

I configured the system for 200,000 bit/s and ran the physical test.

**All 64 packets arrived correctly.**

There were zero detected CRC errors, framing errors, or overruns.

Momo completed every transmission without aborting or reporting a scheduling fault.

I reset both boards and repeated the test.

The second run passed as well.

The combined results were:

| Measurement | Result |
|---|---|
| Nominal signalling rate | 200,000 bit/s |
| Verified packets | 128 / 128 |
| Detected CRC errors | 0 |
| Framing errors | 0 |
| UART overruns | 0 |
| Transmitter aborts | 0 |
| Scheduling faults | 0 |
| Maximum transmitter lateness | 2.5 µs |
| Useful payload throughput | ~18,700 bit/s |

This was a tenfold increase over the 20,000 bit/s signalling rate demonstrated in Experiment 006.

It also demonstrated that the existing laser module could deliver valid packets during these short tests at a rate exceeding its advertised modulation frequency for alternating patterns.

That doesn't mean the laser is guaranteed to operate reliably at this speed over longer durations or under different conditions.

But it was an encouraging result.

I had not yet found the optical hardware's actual speed limit.


## Step 6 — Finally Hit a Limit at 250,000 bit/s

After passing 200,000 bit/s twice, I decided to attempt 250,000 bit/s.

This time, the test failed.

But the reason was interesting.

At 250,000 bit/s, each bit lasts just 4 microseconds.

Momo's software transmitter reached a maximum scheduling lateness of 4 microseconds—an entire bit period.

The firmware detected that it had fallen too far behind schedule and aborted the transmission.

Appa received only one byte, and no complete packet was delivered.

This was different from the timing warnings I had seen in previous tests.

At 57,143, 100,000, and 200,000 bit/s, I had observed maximum transmitter lateness of 2.5 microseconds. That exceeded my separately monitored 2-microsecond target, but the packets still arrived correctly.

At 250,000 bit/s, the transmitter actually fell a full bit behind schedule.

The firmware could no longer reliably generate the intended signal.

**I had finally found a clear software transmitter limitation.**

Importantly, this failure did not demonstrate that my laser or photodiode receiver was incapable of operating at 250,000 bit/s.

The software transmitter failed before the optical hardware could be meaningfully tested at that rate.

To push further, I'll need to change how Momo generates its signal.

## Step 7 — Understanding Signalling Speed vs. Useful Throughput

One of the most interesting observations from this experiment was the difference between raw signalling speed and useful payload throughput.

My nominal signalling rate increased tenfold, from 20,000 to 200,000 bit/s.

But useful payload throughput increased by a much smaller factor.

At 100,000 bit/s, the measured useful payload rate was approximately 15,800 bit/s.

At 200,000 bit/s, it was approximately 18,700 bit/s.

In other words, doubling the signalling rate from 100,000 to 200,000 bit/s increased useful throughput by only about 18%.

Why?

Because my transmission protocol still contains framing overhead and fixed gaps between packets.

As the individual bits become faster, those fixed waiting periods occupy an increasingly large share of the total transmission time.

This gives me two separate opportunities to improve performance.

The first is to increase the underlying signalling speed.

The second is to make better use of the available signalling capacity by reducing unnecessary gaps and improving how packets are transmitted.

Eventually, I'll need to optimize both.

But for now, I have deliberately prioritized discovering the limits of the physical communication system.

## What I Learned

### Dedicated hardware can remove important software limitations

Moving Appa's receiver to hardware UART allowed the microcontroller to handle incoming serial bytes without relying on the previous software-based bit sampling method.

The successful tests demonstrate that hardware UART can receive data through my optical link.

The next logical step is to use hardware UART on the transmitting side as well.

### The fastest-rated component isn't necessarily the first limitation

I originally suspected the laser module's advertised 50 kHz modulation limit might prevent me from reaching 200,000 bit/s.

But the short tests passed.

At 250,000 bit/s, the first demonstrated failure was instead the Arduino's software timing.

This was a useful reminder that the actual bottleneck in a system has to be discovered experimentally.

### Timing measurements need to be interpreted

I initially treated a 2-microsecond transmitter timing target as a strict condition for success.

But multiple tests exceeded that threshold while still delivering every packet correctly.

That didn't mean the timing measurements were unimportant.

It meant I needed to distinguish between an engineering diagnostic threshold and an actual transmission failure.

At 250,000 bit/s, the transmitter reached a full bit period of lateness and aborted.

That was a meaningful failure.

### Short successful tests aren't the same as sustained reliability

The system has now successfully transmitted 128 verified packets at 200,000 bit/s across two separate runs.

That's evidence of repeatable short-burst communication under the tested conditions.

It doesn't establish an error-free link over millions of packets, extended operating periods, changing temperatures, or varying optical alignment.

Longer reliability testing will be an important next step.

### Building real systems teaches me what to build next

Earlier in this project, I was trying to figure out how to turn a laser on and off using an Arduino.

Now I'm investigating UART timing, microcontroller peripherals, packet throughput, optical modulation limits, and custom PCB design.

Each experiment reveals a new constraint and helps me understand what capabilities the next generation of hardware needs.

## Next

The immediate next step is to replace Momo's software-generated transmission with hardware UART transmission.

The Arduino Uno already has a hardware UART transmitter, but its transmit pin is shared with the USB serial connection.

I'll need to address that conflict, along with the laser's control polarity and enable behaviour.

I also have an SN74HC14 logic component available for signal inversion if needed.

My next priorities are:

**1. Hardware UART transmission on Momo**

Move bit generation into dedicated hardware so software scheduling no longer determines the timing of every transmitted bit.

**2. Retest higher signalling rates**

Preserve the working 200,000 bit/s baseline, verify the new hardware transmitter, and then attempt 250,000 and potentially 500,000 bit/s.

**3. Longer reliability testing**

Move beyond short 64-packet bursts to measure performance over larger payloads and longer operating periods.

**4. Improve useful payload throughput**

Investigate reducing fixed packet gaps and improving protocol efficiency.

**5. Custom PCB development**

Begin designing a purpose-built optical communications board in KiCad, potentially using a more capable microcontroller with programmable I/O.

**6. Continue developing the larger system**

Eventually, I want two independent computing devices exchanging real requests and responses through optical links, with automatic pointing, acquisition, and tracking.

The long-term goal remains the same: develop optical networking, internet infrastructure, and computing systems that could eventually operate together in space.
