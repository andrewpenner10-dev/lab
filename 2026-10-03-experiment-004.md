# Experiment 004 — Laser Digital Communication and Speed Testing

Date: October 3, 2026

## Goal

Transmit digital information using the laser for the first time and determine how quickly the current optical receiver can reliably detect the transmitted data.

Experiment 003 established the new laser transmitter and photodetector hardware.

This experiment focused on communication:

Arduino 101 → laser → free space → optical receiver → Arduino 111

The goals were to:

- Encode digital information into the laser beam.
- Synchronize the transmitter and receiver.
- Successfully transmit a complete message.
- Measure the reliability of the link at increasing speeds.
- Find the speed at which the current receiver begins to fail.

I continued referring to the two Arduino boards as:

- **101 — Appa — transmitter**
- **111 — Momo — receiver**

## Step 1 — Return to Digital Communication

The laser was already controllable electronically from Arduino 101.

The next step was to use that control to transmit binary information.

The basic encoding remained simple:

`LASER OFF → 0`

`LASER ON → 1`

Each character is represented by eight binary bits.

Appa turns the laser on and off according to those bits, while Momo samples the optical receiver and reconstructs the transmitted character.

## Step 2 — Synchronization

One of the first problems was timing.

For Momo to correctly interpret the data, it cannot simply detect whether the laser is on or off. It must know when each bit begins and when to sample it.

I added a synchronization signal before each character.

Appa sends:

1. A long laser-ON synchronization pulse.
2. A period of darkness.
3. Eight data bits.

Momo detects the synchronization pulse, waits through the OFF period, and then samples each of the eight data bits near the center of its transmission window.

This allowed the two independent Arduino boards to remain synchronized without an electrical connection between them.

## Step 3 — First Complete Laser Message

The first target message was:

`Hi Momo, my name is Appa. It's nice to meet you. What's your name?`

Early attempts partially worked, but the beginning of the message would be correct while later characters became corrupted.

For example, Momo could correctly receive:

`Hi Momo, my name is Appa...`

before the remaining characters turned into incorrect symbols.

This showed that the optical link itself was working, but small timing errors were accumulating as the message progressed.

The solution was to synchronize again before every character rather than relying on synchronization only at the beginning of the message.

After adjusting the receiver's sampling timing, Momo successfully received the complete message:

`Hi Momo, my name is Appa. It's nice to meet you. What's your name?`

The message crossed the gap entirely through the modulated laser beam.

## Step 4 — Alignment Matters

While debugging the communication system, I discovered another important problem.

Initially, I assumed that if the laser was visibly hitting the receiver, the alignment was good enough.

It wasn't.

I wrote a simple diagnostic program that continuously displayed the receiver's analog reading.

With the laser off, the reading was approximately:

`0`

When the laser only partially illuminated the receiver, readings could be around:

`350`

But when the beam was centered directly on the receiver, the reading reached:

`1023`

The physical position of the beam on the detector therefore made a very large difference to the received signal.

This was an important lesson.

In an optical communications system, pointing the beam at the receiver and pointing it accurately at the receiver are not necessarily the same thing.

From this point forward, I carefully centered the laser before communication testing.

## Step 5 — Build a Communication Benchmark

Once communication was reliable, I wanted something better than simply looking at a sentence and deciding whether it appeared correct.

I changed the system into a benchmark.

Appa transmits 100 known characters.

Momo knows what character it should receive and compares every received character with the expected value.

At the end of each test, Momo reports:

- Bit time
- Raw data rate
- Test distance
- Characters tested
- Correct characters
- Errors
- Character error rate
- PASS or FAIL

This turned the setup from a communication demonstration into a repeatable experiment.

The test distance was:

34.5 cm

## Step 6 — Increase the Speed

I then decreased the amount of time assigned to each individual bit.

The raw data rate is approximately:

`data rate = 1 / bit time`

A 50 ms bit therefore corresponds to:

`20 bits/second`

I progressively reduced the bit time and repeated the 100-character benchmark.

## Results

| Bit Time | Raw Data Rate | Result |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 50 ms | 20 bit/s | 100 / 100 correct |
| 25 ms | 40 bit/s | 100 / 100 correct |
| 15 ms | 66.67 bit/s | 100 / 100 correct |
| 10 ms | 100 bit/s | 100 / 100 correct |
| 7 ms | 142.86 bit/s | 100 / 100 correct |
| 5 ms | 200 bit/s | 100 / 100 correct |
| 4 ms | 250 bit/s | 56 / 100 correct |

The link remained completely error-free through **200 bit/s**.

Then something changed.

At 250 bit/s, only:

56 / 100 characters were correct.

The character error rate jumped to:

44%

## Step 7 — Verify the Failure

Because I had already discovered how important alignment was, I did not immediately assume that the receiver had reached its speed limit.

I carefully repositioned the laser until it was centered directly on the receiver and repeated the 4 ms test.

The result was essentially unchanged.

That made the failure much more interesting.

The link could reliably distinguish the laser at slower speeds, but shortening each bit from 5 ms to 4 ms caused a dramatic increase in errors even with the beam properly aligned.

The current system therefore appears to have crossed a reliability boundary somewhere between:

200 bit/s and 250 bit/s

## Current Record

At a distance of **34.5 cm**:

Fastest demonstrated error-free raw data rate: **200 bit/s**

100 / 100 characters correct

0% character error rate

At 250 bit/s:

56 / 100 characters correct

44% character error rate

The exact boundary has not yet been measured.

## An Interesting Interference Test

During one of the successful tests, I deliberately shone a bright external light at the laser and receiver while data was being transmitted.

The benchmark still reported:

100 / 100 correct

This does not establish how resistant the receiver is to ambient light, but it was interesting that this particular disturbance did not cause a detectable error.

A more controlled interference test will be needed later.

## What I Learned

This experiment taught me considerably more than simply how to make the laser blink faster.

### Synchronization matters

The receiver needs to know not only whether light is present, but when to measure it.

Re-synchronizing before each character prevented timing errors from accumulating across a long message.

### Alignment matters

A beam merely touching the detector can produce a very different signal from a beam centered on it.

Accurate pointing is part of the communications problem.

### Communication performance can be measured

Successfully transmitting one sentence proves that communication is possible.

Sending known test data and counting errors makes it possible to measure how well the link actually performs.

### Faster eventually becomes unreliable

The system did not gradually become slightly worse during these tests.

It remained error-free through 200 bit/s and then produced a large number of errors at 250 bit/s.

Finding that transition experimentally was one of the most interesting results so far.

### A strong signal does not guarantee unlimited speed

When centered, the receiver can show a very strong difference between laser OFF and laser ON.

But signal strength alone does not determine how quickly the detector can respond to changes.

The receiver also needs enough time to transition between optical states.

## Important Limitation

The reported speeds are raw data-bit rates.

They are not the effective message throughput of the complete communication protocol.

The current protocol includes relatively long synchronization periods before each character. Those synchronization periods make the actual end-to-end throughput substantially lower than the raw bit rate.

At this stage, that is intentional.

The purpose of the experiment was to understand and characterize the physical optical link rather than optimize the communication protocol.

There are now several directions to explore.

The immediate experimental question is the exact location of the reliability boundary between 200 and 250 bit/s.

Using microsecond timing instead of whole-millisecond timing would allow tests between 4 ms and 5 ms.

Longer term, the more important next step is improving the receiver.

The current experiment has now demonstrated:

- Laser modulation
- Binary optical communication
- Independent transmitter and receiver synchronization
- Complete text transmission
- Automated error testing
- The importance of beam alignment
- Increasing data rates
- A measurable communication-speed limit

The next receiver architecture should allow the optical link to operate much faster and at greater distances.
