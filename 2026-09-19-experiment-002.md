# Experiment 002 — Sending Digital Data Through Light

**Date:** September 19, 2026

## Goal

Build the first working optical data link between two independent microcontrollers.

The objective was to move beyond simply blinking an LED and demonstrate:

**Arduino TX → light → optical sensor → Arduino RX → decoded data**

## Starting Point

In Experiment 001, I defined the long-term goal of transmitting information between two computers using light.

Since then, I have been building the system one piece at a time.

I deliberately started with a normal green LED and an LDR (light-dependent resistor) instead of a laser and photodiode so I could learn the basic electronics and communication principles first.

## Step 1 — Build the Transmitter

I connected a green LED to an Arduino Uno through a current-limiting resistor.

The first test was simple:

- LED ON for 1 second
- LED OFF for 1 second
- repeat

This proved that the Arduino could control the light source in software.

## Step 2 — Build the Optical Receiver

I connected an LDR to a second Arduino as part of a voltage divider and used the Arduino's analog input to measure the amount of light reaching the sensor.

The receiver could clearly distinguish between different light levels.

This was the first time the system could convert incoming light into an electrical measurement that software could read.

## Step 3 — Turn Light Into Bits

Next, I introduced a threshold.

If the measured light level was above the threshold:

`1`

If it was below the threshold:

`0`

At this point, the system was no longer just measuring brightness. It was interpreting light as digital information.

## Step 4 — Separate TX and RX

I moved the transmitter and receiver onto two independent Arduino Uno boards.

The transmitter controlled the LED.

The receiver monitored the LDR.

There was no electrical data connection between the two systems. The information crossed the gap through light.



https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/bf8ed0f1-e2e3-4eae-a057-f855c21db526




## Step 5 — Transmit a Character

I programmed the transmitter to send:

`01000001`

In binary, this represents the ASCII character:

`A`

The receiver sampled the light, reconstructed the eight bits, converted the byte back into a character, and successfully displayed:

`A`

This was the first successful transmission of encoded digital information through the optical link.

## Step 6 — Synchronization

One of the biggest problems became timing.

If the receiver started reading in the middle of a transmission, it interpreted the correct light pulses as the wrong bits.

I added a preamble — a known bit pattern sent before the actual message.

The receiver searches for this pattern first. Once the preamble is detected, it knows where the message begins.

This changed the system from:

**"Look at the light right now and guess where the data starts."**

to:

**"Search for a known signal, synchronize, then decode the message."**

## Step 7 — Send "HI"

I increased the transmission speed and programmed the transmitter to send two ASCII characters:

`H = 01001000`

`I = 01001001`

The transmitter first sends a preamble and then the 16 data bits.

The receiver searches for the preamble, synchronizes itself to the transmission, reads the incoming bits, reconstructs the two bytes, and converts them back into characters.

Result:

**Received: HI**

Repeatedly.

## Current Prototype

The system can now transmit digital information through free-space light between two independent Arduino boards.

Current architecture:

**Arduino TX → green LED → free-space optical channel → LDR → Arduino RX → serial monitor**

Current demonstrated message:

**HI**

Current data rate:

**~10 bits/second**

Maximum reliable bit rate with LDR receiver: ~21 bits/s (48 ms per bit)

## Problems Discovered

The experiments exposed several real communication problems:

- Receiver and transmitter must be synchronized.
- Ambient light changes the LDR's baseline reading.
- A fixed brightness threshold is fragile.
- Sensor response and sampling speed limit the achievable data rate.
- Physical alignment between the light source and detector matters.

These are now engineering problems to solve rather than unknowns.

## Next

The next experiments will focus on making the link faster and more robust:

- Replace the fixed threshold with adaptive signal detection.
- Test operation under changing ambient light.
- Measure the maximum reliable data rate of the LDR.
- Replace the LDR with a faster photodetector.
- Replace the LED with a more directional optical source.
- Eventually transmit **"HELLO SPACE"** from one computer to another through light.

The basic communication link now works.
